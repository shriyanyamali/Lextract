\title{Lextract: LLM-Based Extraction of Relevant Market Definitions from
European Commission Decisions with Source-Grounded Quality Control}
\runningtitle{Lextract: Market Definition Extraction with Source-Grounded Quality Control}

\author{%
  \fnms{Shriyan S.} \snm{Yamali}%
  \orcid{0009-0002-4861-7264}%
}
\address{Independent Researcher, United States}
\runningauthor{S.S. Yamali}

\begin{abstract}
Relevant market definitions are central to merger and antitrust analysis, but
collecting them at scale from European Commission decisions is difficult because
the decisions are numerous and heterogeneous in structure and wording. We present
Lextract, an open-source pipeline that filters decision PDFs, uses Gemini 2.0
Flash in two calls to locate the market-definition section and to isolate
individual definitions, and stores the results as JSON. Applied to 600 cases, it
produced 4,105 records that feed JurisMercatus, a semantic-search interface. We
document prompts, settings, exclusion rules and thresholds, give a worked
example from source text to record, and combine the model with symbolic quality
control: a structural validity test derived from 50 manually checked
definitions (76.6\% of records pass), a source-fidelity test that checks whether
each record occurs verbatim in its decision, and a manual inspection of 100
records. Checked against all 699 retrievable decisions, 79\% of the 4,067
verifiable records are verbatim and 9\% near-verbatim; 12\% are absent from
their source. Three quarters of the absent records come from short-form
simplified-procedure decisions that evaded the phrase filter, after which the
model fabricated plausible definitions, and 30 are copies of the prompt's own
examples. The structural test accepts 71\% of the absent records and so cannot
detect them; the fidelity test also caught one fabricated record in the
manually checked reference set and yields a cleaned release of 3,607 records. We
describe the fixes and state what remains unmeasured: a gold-standard
precision/recall study and baseline comparisons, for which annotation sheets
and scripts are provided.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

Relevant market definition is a basic step in merger and antitrust analysis: it
fixes the products and the geographic area within which market power is assessed
\cite{marketnotice2024}. Practitioners and researchers therefore need to know how the European
Commission has delineated a given market before: whether retail distribution of
cars is national or EEA-wide, whether a product market was segmented or ``left
open'', and how such findings changed over time. This is a concrete user
problem: the Commission has published more than 6,000 merger and antitrust
decisions \cite{bldr2024} and adds roughly 280 a year \cite{apdtsf2021}; the
market-definition discussion sits under different headings and in different
wording, so keyword search over PDFs is unreliable and manual collection is
slow and hard to reproduce. The only dedicated resource we know of, the Caselex
Market Definitions Module \cite{caselex}, is a paid, editorially curated web
product without bulk or programmatic access.

This paper presents \emph{Lextract},\footnote{\url{https://github.com/shriyanyamali/Lextract/tree/lda2026}}
an open-source pipeline that extracts market-definition passages from
Commission decision PDFs with a large language model (LLM) and stores them as
structured records, and the 4,105-record collection behind
\emph{JurisMercatus},\footnote{\url{https://jurismercatus.shriyanyamali.com}} a
semantic-search interface. The design follows the hybrid view that LLMs are
good at locating and copying legally relevant passages but that their output
must be checked by deterministic means. An earlier version reported only a
structural check and was rightly criticised for not measuring whether extracted
passages are correct. This version contributes:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a complete description of the pipeline (prompts, model and settings,
        exclusion phrases, thresholds) and a worked example from source text to
        record (Sections~\ref{sec:pipeline}--\ref{sec:example});
  \item a description of the released collection and of what researchers can
        do with it (Section~\ref{sec:dataset});
  \item a three-part evaluation: the structural validity test, a new
        \emph{source-fidelity} test that checks whether each record occurs
        verbatim in its decision, and a manual inspection of 100 records
        (Sections~\ref{sec:evaluation}--\ref{sec:results});
  \item an error analysis identifying two failure classes invisible to the
        structural test, fabricated definitions for short-form decisions and
        leakage of prompt examples, with the fixes adopted
        (Section~\ref{sec:errors}), and a statement of what remains unmeasured
        together with the annotation protocol and baseline scripts needed to
        close the gap (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work and Existing Resources}
\label{sec:related-work}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

Legal information extraction combines deterministic, statistical and, more
recently, generative methods. Agnoloni et al.\ presented the BO-ECLI Parser for
extracting and linking legal references from European case law
\cite{agnoloni2017boecli}; Darji et al.\ released annotated German
legal-reference data and stressed the role of domain-specific gold data
\cite{darji2023german}. Recent work moves to semantic structuring with LLMs:
Janatian et al.\ turned legislation into structured pathways for expert systems
\cite{janatian2023text}; Costa et al.\ extracted elements from Brazilian
judicial decisions, evaluated them with precision and recall against expert
annotations and published them with provenance \cite{costa2024semantic}; Spada
et al.\ extracted events and dates from EU procurement notices with expert
validation \cite{spada2025expert}; others address data scarcity and context
\cite{nguyen2025data,bjbmmcmnthsktczmm2025,csfafemspsrdsc2024}. Lextract shares
their pipeline view but targets complete market-definition passages and
emphasises cheap deterministic checks of LLM output, which the hallucination
literature motivates \cite{ktanovssze2025}.

\paragraph{Relation to existing resources.} Caselex \cite{caselex} offers
human-reviewed market definitions across many authorities and languages behind
a subscription and a web interface only. Lextract is not a substitute for that
editorial product; it offers a free, reproducible and updatable collection with
programmatic access: the pipeline can be re-run on new decisions, every record
carries case metadata and the decision URL, and an export tool writes CSV,
JSONL, Parquet, SQLite, BibTeX and RIS. To our knowledge no openly licensed,
machine-readable collection of Commission market definitions existed before.

\section{The Lextract Pipeline}
\label{sec:pipeline}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

Lextract is a sequence of six Python scripts driven by \texttt{run\_pipeline.py}.
The prompts are reproduced verbatim in the repository
(\texttt{docs/PROMPTS.md}) and summarised here.

\subsection{Document Collection and Filtering}

\emph{Step 1} reads the Excel export of the Commission's competition case-search
portal and extracts, per case, the decision URL, case number, year and policy
area (inferred from the URL path). \emph{Step 2} downloads each PDF and extracts
its text with PyPDF2. A document is excluded if it has exactly three pages and
contains one of five phrases from the closing recital of short-form decisions
adopted under the simplified procedure \cite{simplified2013}: ``For the reasons
set out in the Notice on a simplified'', ``European Commission has decided not
to oppose the notified operation'', ``declare it compatible with the internal
market'', ``This decision is adopted in application of Article 6(1)(b)'', and
``Merger Regulation and Article 57 of the EEA Agreement''. Such clearances contain no
market analysis, so any ``definition'' extracted from them would be spurious.
Included documents are
written to batch files with a metadata header and split into two groups at
80,000 characters so that long decisions can be processed separately; no other
length filter is applied. Section~\ref{sec:errors}
shows that this phrase filter was too brittle; the released version normalises
whitespace before matching and applies to documents of at most three pages.

\subsection{Section Identification (LLM call 1)}

\emph{Step 3} sends each batch file to Gemini 2.0 Flash
(\texttt{gemini-2.0-flash} via the \texttt{google-generativeai} SDK with
default generation settings: no temperature, top-$p$ or output-length
override, no system instruction, no JSON mode). The prompt asks the model to
find the first occurrence of the words ``market definition'', to start one line
before it, to continue past sub-headings and to stop at the next heading of the
same level (e.g.\ from ``IV. Market Definition'' to ``V.~\ldots''), excluding
that heading, and to reproduce the metadata header unchanged; the output is the
market-definition section as plain text.

\subsection{Definition Extraction (LLM call 2)}

\emph{Step 4} sends each section to the same model with a prompt that asks for a
JSON array of objects with the fields \texttt{case\_number}, \texttt{year},
\texttt{policy\_area}, \texttt{link}, \texttt{topic} and \texttt{text}. It
instructs the model to extract ``the entirety of each individual market
definition'' as ``a direct quote of the multiple paragraphs. Not a paraphrased
or summarized version''; to treat ``left open'' passages as definitions; to
drop paragraph numbers, footnote markers and footnotes; to re-join words split
by PDF extraction; to invent a short \texttt{topic}; and it states that a
definition ``is always at least 4 sentences long, but never longer than 12
sentences''. It contains four worked examples
(detergents, chemical intermediates, flame retardants, trimethylolpropane). The
two-stage design assumes that the second call performs better on a few pages of
relevant text than on a 50-page decision; this has not been tested against a
single-call design (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Cleaning and Merging}

\emph{Step 5} strips Markdown code fences from the model's output and
\emph{Step 6} concatenates all files into \texttt{data/output.json}. No
de-duplication, truncation or editing of the text is performed, so the released
records are exactly what the model returned.

\section{The JurisMercatus Collection}
\label{sec:dataset}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

The collection (\texttt{data/jurismercatus\_definitions.json} in the
repository) contains 4,105 records from 600 cases (578 merger, 22 antitrust)
and 704 distinct decision documents dated 2016--2025 (Table~\ref{tab:years}).
The documents were exported from
the case-search portal in English; the exact export query of the original run
was not recorded, which we note as a reproducibility gap, but the released
links identify the processed documents exactly. Cases contribute 1 to 45 records
(median 6); records range from 9 to 1,182 words (median 67, mean 91). The \texttt{topic} field holds 3,250
distinct free-text labels. The collection is not de-duplicated: 117 records
repeat the text of another record, 89 of them within the same case (one passage
emitted under two topic labels). PDF-extraction artefacts such as ``in -house''
survive in 30\% of records. A cleaned release
(\texttt{data/jurismercatus\_definitions\_clean.json}; 3,607 records, 455
cases) drops the records that the fidelity test (Section~\ref{sec:results})
finds absent from their source and all records from documents of at most three
pages.

\paragraph{What researchers can do with it.} Each record pairs a passage with
its case number, year, policy area and decision URL, so it can be cited and
checked. Through JurisMercatus, a query such as ``retail distribution of new
passenger cars, geographic scope'' returns the passages in which the Commission
discussed that question across cases, ranked by embedding similarity.
Exported to a
spreadsheet or SQLite database, the records support questions such as how often
the Commission leaves a definition open by sector and year, or how geographic
findings (national, EEA-wide, worldwide) are distributed.

\section{Evaluation Design}
\label{sec:evaluation}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Reference Set and Structural Validity Test}

No gold standard listing all market definitions in a set of decisions exists,
so we use three deliberately limited instruments and say what each measures.
The reference set (\texttt{evaluation/reference.json}) holds 50 records that
the author selected from the pipeline output and checked against the source
PDFs: a convenience sample, not a random one, of 39 cases, all merger
decisions, mostly 2022--2024, 33 to 557 words long (median 135). Re-examining
it for this paper revealed one duplicated record, two case numbers without the
``M.'' prefix (one mistyped) and, through the fidelity test, one record that
does not occur in its source (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

The structural test (\texttt{evaluate.py}) derives three checks from the
reference set. \emph{Length}: 26 to 668 words, the reference range widened by
20\% on each side. \emph{Legal phrase}: at least one of the 121 word $n$-grams
($n=2$--5) that occur in at least 15\% of reference records (e.g.\ ``can be
left open'', ``for the purposes of this decision''; many are function-word
sequences such as ``of the''). \emph{Structural marker}: at least one of 18
fixed markers: ``relevant market'', ``product market'', ``geographic market'',
``market definition'', ``left open'', ``EEA-wide'', ``EEA wide'', ``national
market'', ``Commission considers'', ``Commission found'', ``Commission has
previously'', ``Notifying Party'', ``internal market'', ``serious doubts'',
``plausible market'', ``exact scope'', ``for the purposes of'', ``cannot be
excluded''. Seven of the 50 reference records fail the marker check
themselves, so the markers are a strict rather than a lenient proxy.
\emph{What it measures:} whether a record resembles a verified
market-definition passage in length and phrasing. \emph{What it does not
measure:} whether the text occurs in the decision (fabrication), whether its
boundaries are right, whether it is the right passage, or whether definitions
were missed. We report a \emph{structural validity rate}, not accuracy.

\subsection{Source-Fidelity Test}

The fidelity test (\texttt{verify\_fidelity.py}) asks whether a record is
copied from its decision. Record and PyPDF2 decision text are normalised to
lower-case letters only, which removes whitespace, digits and punctuation and so
neutralises paragraph numbers, footnote markers and split-word artefacts. The
record is cut into 40-letter windows at stride 20, and \emph{coverage} is the
fraction of windows found in the source: \emph{verbatim} ($\ge 0.95$),
\emph{near-verbatim} (0.80--0.95), \emph{partial} (0.50--0.80) or \emph{absent}
($<0.50$). The test cannot distinguish a copy from text differing only in digits
or punctuation, nor judge whether the copied passage is the \emph{right} one.
It was run on every record whose decision could be fetched from the stored URL
in October 2026: 699 of the 704 documents and 4,067 of the 4,105 records (four
URLs of case M.11241 now redirect and one points to an HTML index page).

\subsection{Manual Inspection}

To learn what the structural test's verdicts mean, 50 passing and 50 failing
records were drawn at random (seeded) and read. Each was labelled from the
record text alone, without the decision, as A1: a market-definition statement
(precedent, party submission, market-investigation finding or Commission
conclusion on product or geographic scope); A2: reasoning within a
market-definition analysis without a definitional claim; B: a fragment such as
a bare heading; or C: not market definition (competitive assessment, remedies,
party description, legal background). The labels are released with the sample
(\texttt{evaluation/manual\_inspection\_sample.csv}). This is a
single-annotator face-validity reading, not a gold standard, and borderline
cases exist: a conclusion that a transaction raises no serious doubts
``regardless of the exact product market definition'' was labelled C, a list
of ``affected markets'' A1. Precision, recall and F1 require, for whole
decisions, the list of every passage that counts as a definition, and what
counts, at which unit (paragraph, sub-section, conclusion only), changes the
scores. The repository contains an annotation protocol and a script that
produces, for $N$ random decisions, a sheet listing every record next to the
decision text with columns for true positive, boundary error, false positive
and missed definition (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Structural Validity}

Of the 4,105 records, 3,143 (76.6\%) pass all three checks. The 962 failures
decompose as 668 marker only, 185 length only, 80 length and marker, 19 phrase
and marker, and 10 all three; of the 275 length failures, 261 are shorter than
26 words and 14 longer than 668. The yearly rate (Table~\ref{tab:years}) lies
between 68\% and 86\% in every year with more than 50 records except 2024
(62\%); the 65 antitrust records also reach only 62\%. Among the 777 records without a marker,
535 (69\%) still contain the word ``market'', 105 ``national'' and 65 ``EEA'',
i.e.\ many express scope in wording the marker list does not cover (``the
market is national in scope''); 242 (31\%) do not mention a market.

Of the 4,067 verifiable records (Table~\ref{tab:fidelity}), 3,221 (79.2\%) are
verbatim, 349 (8.6\%) near-verbatim, 26 (0.6\%) partial and 471 (11.6\%)
absent. Absence is
concentrated in documents that contain no market analysis: 136 of the 699
documents have at most three pages, and 353 of their 380 records are absent
(the other 27 are copied boilerplate such as party descriptions). Of the 118
absent records from longer documents, 26 are copies of the prompt's own
examples (Section~\ref{sec:errors}); 32 come from twelve documents of four to
seven pages, eleven of which yielded no copied record at all; and 60 come from
19 longer documents: ancillary decisions that refer back to a main clearance
decision, whose records restate the main decision's definitions in words that
do not occur in the document, and a few long decisions whose records share only
fragments with the text (coverage below 0.5). Of the 349 near-verbatim records, 292 have at most
two missing windows, i.e.\ a single break; inspection of such cases in twelve
documents found two causes, neither of them paraphrase: footnote text
interleaved at a page break in the PyPDF2 text (as in the worked example), and
a skipped intermediate paragraph (e.g.\ a party's submission between the
Commission's precedent and its conclusion in M.10745).

Of the 48 reference records whose documents could be retrieved, 44 are verbatim,
three near-verbatim and one absent: the record for M.10993, a three-page short-form decision, consists of plausible
telecommunications market-definition language that does not occur in the
decision. The manual check behind the reference set thus missed one fabricated
record. The structural test does not detect fabrication: 336 of the
471 absent records pass it (71\%), close to the rate for verbatim records
(2,475 of 3,221, 77\%); fabricated passages are, by construction, well-formed
Commission prose.

\subsection{Manual Inspection}

Of the 50 records that pass the structural test, 49 were labelled A1 and one C
(a competitive-assessment conclusion); one of the 49 is, however, a leaked
prompt example (Section~\ref{sec:errors}): face-valid and well-formed, but not
from the decision it is attributed to. Of the 50 failing records, 38 were
labelled A1 and three A2, i.e.\ 41 (82\%; interval 69--90\%) are
market-definition content; one is a bare heading (B) and eight (16\%) are not
market definitions (C): remedies reasoning, a party description, Treaty text,
and competitive assessment. Seven of the 41 are complete one-sentence scope
statements below the 26-word minimum, such as ``For AIs, the Commission
therefore takes the view that the markets are at least EEA-wide''; four of them
fail on length alone. One is a coherent 727-word party submission that fails
only the maximum. The structural test's failures are thus mostly wording and length
mismatches, as reviewers suspected, and its passes mostly genuine definitions;
neither verdict is evidence about fidelity.

\section{Error Analysis and Fixes}
\label{sec:errors}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph{Short-form decisions that evade the filter.} Most absent records, 353
of 471, come from simplified-procedure clearances. These contain the exclusion phrases, but
PyPDF2 renders them with inserted spaces and line breaks (``on a simpli fied
procedure'', ``Merger\,\,Regulation''), so exact matching fails: in a 40-document
pilot sample, none of eight three-page documents matched a phrase, and the two-page document that did was not excluded because
the rule applied only to three-page documents. The documents then reached call 1, whose prompt
presupposes a market-definition section, and call 2, whose prompt says ``you
will see many market definitions''. For a decision with no market analysis the
model produced precedent-style passages that read like Commission text; eight
records in the collection are the simplified-procedure recital itself. The released code normalises
whitespace before matching and applies the rule to documents of at most three
pages, which excludes all nine short-form documents of the pilot; the fidelity
script drops absent records and, optionally, every record from documents of at
most three pages, which produced the cleaned release.

\paragraph{Prompt-example leakage.} The four example passages in the extraction
prompt were emitted as records for decisions that are not their source: 30
records across 11 cases reproduce example text verbatim. They pass the structural test and a face-validity reading; the
fidelity test rejects all 30. The examples will be removed from the prompt; the
effect on extraction quality is untested.

\paragraph{Boundaries, duplicates and wording.} Near-verbatim records show the
model splicing across footnotes and occasionally omitting a paragraph; length
failures show the opposite boundary problem (261 records under 26 words, mostly
single conclusion sentences and seven bare headings; 14 over 668 words); 117
exact duplicates mostly reflect one passage emitted under two topics. These are
segmentation issues, not fidelity failures, for a gold-standard study to score.
Most remaining structural failures are genuine definitions in wording the 18
markers do not cover, notably ``national in scope''; extending the marker list
would raise the rate without changing the collection, so we report the manual
inspection instead.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

\emph{No gold standard.} Precision, recall and F1 against an independently
annotated set of all definitions in a decision have not been measured: the
fidelity test shows that 88\% of records are copied from their source, not
that they are the right passages or that none were missed. The
sheet and protocol in \texttt{make\_annotation\_sheet.py} are ready for an
expert annotation of ten decisions. \emph{No baselines.} The two-stage
design, the choice of Gemini 2.0 Flash (taken for its free-tier rate limits and
long context) and the claim that rule-based extraction is brittle are stated,
not demonstrated. A single-call variant and a heading-based rule baseline
(\texttt{baselines/}) feed the same evaluation scripts, but neither they nor
other LLMs have been run yet. \emph{Single
annotation.} The manual inspection and the reference set were each labelled
once, without a second, independent annotator. \emph{Scope.} Only English-language decisions of one authority were
processed, and PDF text quality bounds output quality.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

Lextract turns heterogeneous Commission decisions into 4,105 structured
market-definition records with a two-call LLM pipeline whose prompts, settings
and filters are now fully documented. The joint lesson of the structural test,
the fidelity test and the manual reading is the one the workshop's call
anticipates: the LLM copies the right kind of passage most of the time (79\%
verbatim, 9\% near-verbatim across the collection), but it also fabricates fluent
definitions when handed a decision that has none and leaks prompt examples into
the output, and a resemblance-based check sees neither failure. A deterministic
check against the source text does, at negligible cost, and it also caught a
fabricated record in a manually verified reference set. We regard such a test
as a necessary component of any LLM extraction pipeline over legal documents.
